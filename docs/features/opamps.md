# Operational amplifier stages — executable feature catalog

## Scope, evidence and implementation contract

This lane owns eight distinct physical stage-design problems: inverting amplification, noninverting amplification, unequal-ratio differential subtraction, weighted summation, active integration, active differentiation, sinusoidal slew-rate sizing, and finite-open-loop dominant-pole bandwidth. These are not eight rearrangements of one equation. The first four use different signal connections/loading or multiple-source operations; the next two use different capacitor placements and time-domain operations; the last two address different amplifier limitations (large-signal slope versus small-signal loop gain).

No passive resonance, analog filter synthesis, noise budgets, ADC performance calculation, or passive RC charging calculation is included. “Noise gain” in calculator 8 means reciprocal feedback factor, not a noise calculation. Integrator and differentiator models are explicitly local/finite-interval ideal stage models, not claims that a real uncompensated circuit has ideal response at every frequency.

Research was conducted on 2026-10-09 using web searches restricted to manufacturer application reports, handbooks and tutorials. Terms covered inverting/noninverting/summing/difference stages, integrator/differentiator equations, sinusoidal slew rate, single-pole open-loop gain and finite-gain closed-loop bandwidth. English-language accessible primary material was included, with no publication-date restriction because the circuit laws are not technology-specific. Four retrieved TI documents below provide the final evidence. Analog Devices tutorials/FAQ surfaced in search but direct retrieval failed; they are not treated as inspected evidence. An initially returned SLAA416A document concerns high-speed ADC interfaces rather than the advertised slew-rate tutorial and was not used. This is a focused engineering reference synthesis, not an exhaustive systematic literature review.

## Shared model and safety domains

For calculators 1–6, use an ideal op amp in stable negative feedback: zero input current, effectively infinite loop gain, and a virtual equality of input-pin voltages. This equality does not permit exceeding the real input common-mode range. Source voltage and op-amp pin voltage are not necessarily the same.

The catalog therefore asks for `v_cm_min`, `v_cm_max`, `v_out_min`, and `v_out_max`. These must be actual guaranteed usable device ranges, not blindly copied supply rails. The worked examples use input-pin and output ranges of **−10 V to +10 V**, ordered strictly. Those numbers are illustrative limits, not the specifications of a claimed part. Appropriate supplies, loading and output drive must be selected separately. Each catalog constrains its calculated pin and output voltages to those ranges. Saturation is rejected rather than pretending to clip an ideal result into a valid answer.

All resistors used as divisors are strictly positive. Positive capacitance is required for the integrator/differentiator. Reference voltages are held constant, and the models neglect input offset, bias-current error, finite-loop-gain error, parasitics, component tolerance except where actual unequal resistances are entered, and output-current limits. Calculations 1–4 are DC or sufficiently slow signals; calculations 5–6 require adequate local bandwidth/slew performance. These assumptions are real model boundaries rather than claims of universal accuracy.

## 1. `opamps.inverting_stage` — basic

### Physical problem and connections

Find the signed gain and DC output of an inverting stage. `r_in` includes source resistance and joins `v_in` to the inverting input; `r_feedback` joins output to that node. The noninverting input is held at `v_reference`.

### Derivation

Negative feedback makes the summing node approximately `Vref`. Zero input current gives

\[
\frac{V_{in}-V_{ref}}{R_{in}}+\frac{V_o-V_{ref}}{R_f}=0.
\]

Thus

\[
G=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}},\qquad V_o=V_{ref}+G(V_{in}-V_{ref}).
\]

Gain is V/V; resistances are ohms and voltages volts. The reference-aware result follows directly from H pp. 18–19 and the virtual-short/KCL treatment in S section 3; it is not a guessed rail-offset formula.

### Domain and limitations

Both resistors are positive. The input-pin common-mode voltage is `Vref`, **not** `Vin`. Validate `Vref` against the supplied common-mode range and the calculated `Vo` against the output range. The source sees approximately `Rin`, unlike the high-input-impedance noninverting stage. Slow-signal and output-drive assumptions remain external.

### Independent example — all expected outputs

`Rin=10,000 ohm`, `Rf=47,000 ohm`, `Vin=0.2 V`, `Vref=0 V`; common-mode/output limits both ±10 V.

- `gain = -47000/10000 = -4.7 V/V`.
- Input current is independently `0.2/10000 = 20 microA`; feedback must carry the opposite current.
- `v_out = -20 microA * 47000 ohm = -0.94 V`.

Pin voltage 0 V and output −0.94 V are inside their ranges. A nonzero reference is handled by differences from `Vref`, not by simply adding a reference to a ground-referred answer.

## 3. `opamps.difference_ratio_error` — expert

### Physical problem and connections

Predict differential gain and external common-mode leakage when a four-resistor difference stage does **not** have perfectly matched resistor ratios. `R1` joins `V1` to the inverting input; `R2` is feedback; `R3` joins `V2` to the noninverting input; `R4` joins that pin to `Vref`.

This is one generalized difference-amplifier calculator; matched and mismatched variants are not counted as separate problems. H pp. 24–25 uses divider analysis and superposition; H pp. 74–75 discusses difference stages and common-mode rejection. The unequal-value extension here is derived explicitly rather than assuming ratio matching.

### Derivation

Let

\[
k=\frac{R_2}{R_1},\qquad p=\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}.
\]

Zero noninverting-input current gives `Vpin=p V2+(1-p)Vref`. Inverting-node KCL then yields

\[
V_o=(1+k)V_{pin}-kV_1.
\]

Define **source** common-mode and differential voltages as

\[
V_{cm}=(V_1+V_2)/2,\quad V_d=V_2-V_1,
\quad V_1=V_{cm}-V_d/2,\quad V_2=V_{cm}+V_d/2.
\]

Substitution gives

\[
V_o=A_d V_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}+(1+k)(1-p)V_{ref},
\]

\[
A_d=\frac{(1+k)p+k}{2},\qquad A_{cm}=(1+k)p-k.
\]

When `R2/R1=R4/R3=k`, `p=k/(1+k)`, so `Ad=k`, `Acm=0`, and `Vo=Vref+k(V2-V1)`. No CMRR output is formed: exact matching would make a CMRR division singular. A zero common-mode gain is a valid finite output.

### Domain and limitations

All four resistors are positive. The actual op-amp **pin** common-mode voltage is `Vpin`; it is generally not `(V1+V2)/2`. Validate `Vpin` and `Vo` against their entered ranges. `Acm` is only resistor-network leakage; intrinsic op-amp CMRR, finite gain and bias errors are excluded. Input source impedances must be negligible or included in `R1`/`R3`. Actual resistor values can represent a mismatch scenario, not a worst-case tolerance distribution.

### Independent example — all expected outputs

`R1=10,000 ohm`, `R2=100,000 ohm`, `R3=10,000 ohm`, `R4=101,000 ohm`, `V1=1 V`, `V2=1.1 V`, `Vref=0 V`; both operating ranges ±10 V.

Here `k=10`, `p=101/111`, and `(1+k)p=1111/111=10+1/111`.

- `differential_gain = 10+1/222 = 10.004504504504505 V/V`.
- `common_mode_gain = 1/111 = 0.009009009009009009 V/V`.
- `pin_voltage = (101/111)*(11/10) = 1111/1110 = 1.000900900900901 V`.
- `v_out = 11*(1111/1110)-10 = 1121/1110 = 1.0099099099099098 V`.

An independent decomposition uses `Vd=0.1 V`, `Vcm=1.05 V`: differential contribution is approximately 1.00045045 V and common-mode leakage 0.00945946 V, summing to the same 1.00990991 V. Both pin and output domains pass. The 1% change of R4 produces measurable leakage despite an otherwise ideal op amp.

### Implementation conditioning correction

Let `q=R4/R3`, so `p=q/(1+q)`. Algebra gives

\[
A_{cm}=(1+k)\frac{q}{1+q}-k=\frac{q-k}{1+q}.
\]

The executable expression uses `(r_four/r_three-r_two/r_one)/(1+r_four/r_three)`. It subtracts identical computed ratios directly, preserving exact zero at ratio matching rather than subtracting two separately rounded amplified divider terms. No CMRR division or special-case clamp is added. Differential gain, pin/output equations, nominal expected values and sequential output order remain unchanged. Tiny physical mismatches cannot be resolved below the floating-point precision of the supplied resistor values; this is not a tolerance-statistics model. Parent/independent QA should cover matched noninteger ratios and both mismatch signs. No such checks are executed here.

## 8. `opamps.dominant_pole_gain_bandwidth` — expert

### Physical problem

Estimate the closed-loop pole, low-frequency gain deficit, and conventional GBW/noise-gain approximation of a resistive-feedback voltage-feedback amplifier. This accounts for finite open-loop gain rather than merely repeating calculator 2's ideal resistor gain.

### Definition of GBW

Use the dominant-pole model

\[
A(s)=\frac{A_0}{1+s/\omega_p},\quad \omega_p=2\pi f_p.
\]

Here the entered `gain_bandwidth` is the asymptotic product `A0 fp`. For a single pole,

\[
|A(j2\pi f)|=\frac{A_0}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_p)^2}},
\]

so the exact unity-gain crossing is `fp sqrt(A0^2-1)`, **not** exactly `A0 fp`. They approach equality for large A0. S section 5.16 explicitly distinguishes unity-gain bandwidth and GBW. B section 4 uses the conventional high-A0 unity-frequency parameterization; this catalog labels the asymptotic product explicitly so finite-gain algebra stays consistent. No exact unity-crossing result is separately counted as another calculator.

### Derivation

Suppress the independent source to find the resistive feedback factor:

\[
\beta=\frac{R_g}{R_f+R_g},\qquad N=1/\beta=1+R_f/R_g.
\]

For the noninverting/noise-path transfer,

\[
H(s)=\frac{A(s)}{1+\beta A(s)}
=\frac{A_0}{1+A_0/N+s/\omega_p}.
\]

Putting this in normalized one-pole form gives

\[
G_{dc}=\frac{A_0}{1+A_0/N},\qquad
f_{cl}=f_p(1+A_0/N),\qquad f_p=GBW/A_0.
\]

The closed-loop bandwidth is the minus-3-dB frequency **relative to its actual finite DC gain**. For large loop gain,

\[
f_{cl}\approx GBW/N.
\]

The fractional gain deficit relative to ideal noninverting gain N is

\[
1-\frac{G_{dc}}{N}=\frac{1}{1+A_0/N}.
\]

Using this direct reciprocal rather than subtracting two near-equal floats avoids unnecessary numerical cancellation. B section 4 supplies the single-pole conventional feedback derivation; S sections 2.1 and 5.16 establish finite-loop-gain and specification definitions.

For an inverting signal source through `Rg`, the signed DC signal gain is instead

\[
G_{inv,dc}=-\frac{R_f/R_g}{1+N/A_0},
\]

while its denominator and bandwidth use the **same noise gain N**. Do not divide GBW by the magnitude of inverting signal gain. `dc_gain` in the catalog is explicitly the noninverting/noise-path gain, not that signed inverting gain.

### Domain and limitations

`Rg>0`, `Rf>=0`, `A0>1`, `GBW>0`. The minimum stable noise gain is entered from the device documentation and must be at least 1; enforce `1+Rf/Rg >= minimum_stable_noise_gain`. Source resistance belongs in `Rg` after independent sources are suppressed.

The finite-gain formulas are exact **within this single-pole model**; the `approximate_bandwidth` output requires high loop gain for accuracy. A low A0/N does not create a division singularity; its large `relative_gain_error` is the appropriate warning. Decompensated amplifiers may require a minimum noise gain. The extra constraint is necessary, not sufficient, for real stability: additional poles, capacitive loading, source/feedback reactance and layout parasitics are not represented. Current-feedback amplifiers, large-signal slew limits and active phase-correction schemes are excluded. A0 and GBW must correspond to compatible supply/load/temperature conditions.

### Independent example — all expected outputs

`Rg=10,000 ohm`, `Rf=90,000 ohm`, `A0=100,000 V/V`, `GBW=10,000,000 Hz`, `minimum_stable_noise_gain=1 V/V`.

- `noise_gain = 1+90000/10000 = 10 V/V`.
- `open_loop_pole = 10000000/100000 = 100 Hz`.
- Loop gain at DC is `A0/N=10000`.
- `dc_gain = 100000/10001 = 9.99900009999 V/V`.
- `closed_loop_bandwidth = 100*(1+10000) = 1,000,100 Hz`.
- `approximate_bandwidth = 10000000/10 = 1,000,000 Hz`.
- `relative_gain_error = 1/10001 = 0.00009999000099990002`, approximately 0.009999%.

The exact DC-gain/pole product is `100000/10001 * (100*10001) = 10,000,000 Hz`, an independent algebraic cross-check. Noise gain 10 exceeds the entered stability minimum 1. All example values are finite and all denominators positive.

## 2. `opamps.noninverting_stage` — basic

### Physical problem and connections

Find the gain and reference-shifted output when `Vin` directly drives the noninverting pin. `Rg` connects the inverting node to `Vref`; `Rf` is output-to-inverting-node feedback. The `r_ground` key names `Rg`, even when the reference is nonzero.

### Derivation

With `Vminus=Vin`, KCL at the inverting node gives

\[
\frac{V_{in}-V_{ref}}{R_g}+\frac{V_{in}-V_o}{R_f}=0,
\quad
V_o=V_{in}+\frac{R_f}{R_g}(V_{in}-V_{ref})
=V_{ref}+\left(1+\frac{R_f}{R_g}\right)(V_{in}-V_{ref}).
\]

The limiting `Rf=0` case is a unity buffer, permitted by the catalog. The output expression remains finite without evaluating the KCL form's intermediate division by zero. H p. 17 and S section 2 establish the noninverting feedback relationship.

### Unity-follower conditioning and independent regression vector

The executable output and both output-swing guards use `v_in+(r_feedback/r_ground)*(v_in-v_reference)`. Separating the direct input contribution avoids erasing a tiny `Vin` when `Rf=0` and a much larger reference makes `Vin-Vref` round to `-Vref`. The algebraic gain, reference topology and admissible domain are unchanged; no special case or clamp is used.

## 4. `opamps.weighted_summing_stage` — intermediate

### Physical problem and connections

Find three independently adjustable voltage weights and their algebraic summed output. Each source has its own input resistor into the inverting node; `Rf` is feedback and the noninverting input is `Vref`. This is not a passive short between voltage sources.

### Derivation

The virtual summing node is `Vref`. Applying KCL,

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{V_i-V_{ref}}{R_i}+\frac{V_o-V_{ref}}{R_f}=0.
\]

Consequently,

\[
G_i=-R_f/R_i,\qquad
V_o=V_{ref}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}G_i(V_i-V_{ref}).

H pp. 23–24 explicitly derives the adder and scaling summer. The nonzero-reference form follows from the same node equation. Each source sees its own resistor while currents sum at a feedback-maintained potential.

## 5. `opamps.integrator_constant_input` — intermediate

### Physical problem and connections

Calculate an active integrator's constant-input output ramp over a finite interval, starting from a known output/capacitor state. The input element is `R`; the feedback element is `C`; the noninverting pin is a constant reference. This is an op-amp computation stage, not passive exponential capacitor charging.

### Derivation

The input current is `(Vin-Vref)/R`. Feedback-capacitor current leaving the summing node is `C d(Vref-Vo)/dt = -C dVo/dt`. KCL therefore gives

\[
\frac{dV_o}{dt}=-\frac{V_{in}-V_{ref}}{RC}.
\]

Integrating a constant input over duration `t` gives

\[
V_o(t)=V_{initial}-\frac{V_{in}-V_{ref}}{RC}t.
\]

`RC` has seconds units, so the slope is V/s and the endpoint is V. H p. 21 establishes the integrator relation; pp. 55–58 explain practical integrator concerns.

### Domain and limitations

`R>0`, `C>0`, `duration>=0`. Both initial and final output must be within the actual output range. Because the modeled slope is constant, endpoint checks cover the full interval; a zero-duration case is valid. The reference must be inside the input common-mode range. Initial charge/reset must be known. No parallel leakage resistor, capacitor leakage, bias/offset drift, reset transient or arbitrary waveform integration is modeled. A practical integrator may require reset or DC stabilization; adequate slew rate and loop gain are separate prerequisites.

### Independent example — all expected outputs

`R=10,000 ohm`, `C=100 nF=1e-7 F`, `Vin=0.25 V`, `Vref=0 V`, `Vinitial=1 V`, `duration=2 ms=0.002 s`; ranges ±10 V.

`RC=0.001 s`; input current is 25 microA.

- `output_slope = -0.25/0.001 = -250 V/s`.
- The capacitor receives `25 microA * 2 ms = 50 nC`, corresponding to a 0.5 V change on 100 nF.
- `v_final = 1-0.5 = 0.5 V`.

Both output endpoints (1 and 0.5 V) and the 0 V pin voltage pass their domains.

## 6. `opamps.differentiator_ramp` — intermediate

Differentiation and integration are different capacitor placements and physical operations, not inverse rearrangements of a scalar gain formula.

The source ramp slope is

\[

\]

The capacitor injects `I=C m` into the virtual-reference node. Resistor-feedback KCL gives

\[
V_o=V_{ref}-R_f C m.
\]

`Rf C` has seconds units, which multiplies the V/s slope to produce volts. H pp. 21–22 provides the capacitor-current derivation and cautions that this is particularly difficult to realize ideally. Practical differentiators/limitations are discussed at pp. 38–39 and 61–62.

Ideal abrupt ramp corners or input steps would demand transients that this local model does not calculate. Use it only after startup and away from corners where the practical compensated stage approximates differentiation. ## 7. `opamps.sinusoidal_slew_requirement` — intermediate

### Physical problem

Size the output slew-rate capability needed to reproduce a given sine-wave amplitude and frequency, distinguishing the mathematical slope bound from a user-selected engineering margin. This is a large-signal limitation, not small-signal bandwidth.

### Derivation

For

\[
V_o(t)=V_{center}+V_{peak}\sin(2\pi f t),
\quad
\frac{dV_o}{dt}=2\pi fV_{peak}\cos(2\pi f t),
\]

the largest slope magnitude is

\[
SR_{required}=2\pi fV_{peak}.
\]

With a chosen multiplier `M`, `SRdesign=M SRrequired`; available theoretical margin is `SRavailable/SRrequired`. Equation 13 and section 6.1(a) of R explicitly use the sine-wave slope relation; S section 5.13 defines slew rate. R warns that low distortion requires additional margin, whose amount depends on the application.

### Units, domain and interpretation

Input/output slew rates are **V/s**, not V/microsecond. Multiply a datasheet V/microsecond rating by `1e6` before entering it. `Vpeak` is an **output** peak amplitude, not RMS, peak-to-peak or an input amplitude. `f>0`, `Vpeak>0`, `SRavailable>0`, `M>=1`; the positive frequency/amplitude guard makes the reported margin division well-defined. A DC/zero-amplitude case is outside this particular nonzero-sinusoid calculator.

Validate `Vcenter-Vpeak` and `Vcenter+Vpeak` against actual output-swing limits. Use the smaller guaranteed positive/negative slew-rate magnitude. A margin below 1 flags a theoretically slew-limited requested waveform; a margin between 1 and `M` falls short of the requested design margin. These are useful diagnostic outputs, not invalid mathematical inputs, so the catalog does not reject insufficient available slew rate. The catalog does reject insufficient output swing because its assumed sinusoidal amplitude could not be reproduced linearly.

No universal low-distortion guarantee is assigned to a 2× multiplier. Bandwidth, output current, stability, common-mode compliance and settling remain separate checks. Differential slew-rate specifications cannot be substituted blindly into a single-ended output calculation.

### Independent example — all expected outputs

`f=100,000 Hz`, `Vpeak=2 V`, `Vcenter=0 V`, `SRavailable=5,000,000 V/s`, `M=2`; output limits ±10 V.

- `design_slew = 2,513,274.1228718343 V/s`.
- `slew_margin = 5,000,000/(400,000*pi) = 3.978873577297384`.

The desired sine lies between −2 and +2 V, so swing constraints pass. The available slew exceeds the chosen 2× design target; actual distortion remains device-dependent.
